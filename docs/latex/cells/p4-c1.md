# P4 C1 — Three disjoint congruence classes

**Solved — complete written proof.** For any three pairwise disjoint integer congruence classes with positive moduli, some pair of moduli has greatest common divisor at least $3$.

For an integer $a$ and a positive integer $m$, write

$$
a\pmod m=\{a+mt:t\in\mathbb Z\}.
$$

Repeated moduli and residues are allowed. We first establish the precise intersection test used below. Let $a\pmod m$ and $b\pmod n$ be two classes, and set $g=\gcd(m,n)$. They intersect if and only if $g$ divides $b-a$. Indeed, if an integer $z$ lies in both classes, then $m\mid z-a$ and $n\mid z-b$. Since $g$ divides both $m$ and $n$, it divides

$$
(z-a)-(z-b)=b-a.
$$

Conversely, suppose $b-a=gt$. Bézout's identity gives integers $x,y$ with $g=mx+ny$. Set $z=a+mxt$. Then $z-a=mxt$ is divisible by $m$, while

$$
z-b=mxt-(b-a)=mxt-(mx+ny)t=-nyt
$$

is divisible by $n$. Hence $z$ lies in both classes. This proves the intersection test, including when the moduli are equal.

Now let $a_0\pmod{m_0}$, $a_1\pmod{m_1}$, and $a_2\pmod{m_2}$ be pairwise disjoint. Assume, toward a contradiction, that every pair of moduli has gcd less than $3$. Every such gcd is positive. It cannot equal $1$: by the intersection test, a pair with coprime moduli would intersect because $1$ divides every integer. Therefore

$$
\gcd(m_0,m_1)=\gcd(m_0,m_2)=\gcd(m_1,m_2)=2.
$$

For each pair $i\ne j$, disjointness and the intersection test imply $2\nmid a_j-a_i$. Thus $a_0,a_1,a_2$ are pairwise distinct modulo $2$. There are only two residue classes modulo $2$, so this is impossible. Consequently at least one pair satisfies $\gcd(m_i,m_j)\ge3$, as required.

The bound is attained: $0\pmod3$, $1\pmod3$, and $2\pmod3$ are pairwise disjoint, and every pair of their moduli has gcd exactly $3$.
